Publication

American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, vol. 17, no. 3 (2025), pp. 345–372.

Large-scale quasi-experimental evaluation

Research Question

Does a one-year national Lesson study–style mathematics PD program (the Boost for Mathematics) improve student mathematics achievement, and how does it change teacher training, peer collaboration, and self-reported classroom practices?

Sample / Evidence Base

  • Country: Sweden (compulsory school).
  • Education level: Grades 3, 6, 9 (lower/middle/higher stage exam endpoints).
  • Subject: Mathematics.
  • Student analytic sample: Up to 2,874,158 student observations (Table 2); 959–1,331 schools in descriptive Table 1 depending on participation wave.
  • Teacher survey: ~7,819–8,384 observations in mechanism tables (Tables 5–7).
  • Timeline: Program waves 2013, 2014, 2015; long-run follow-up up to 4–5 years post-implementation for some cohorts.

---

Student Achievement Measure

Subject: Mathematics (national centralized exams at grades 3, 6, 9).

Scoring: Test scores standardized by year in the full population (mean 0, SD 1 within year).

Assignment: Students sampled at beginning of stage (grades 1/4/6) and assigned expected school’s treatment status at end of stage (grades 3/6/9)—reduced-form ITT (~84% attend expected school; authors discuss scaling but primary estimate remains reduced-form).

Primary pooled post-implementation effect: +0.0263 SD (SE 0.0085, Table 2 Panel B).

Heterogeneity: Null/small for lowest quartile of predicted scores; positive for upper quartiles (Table 4). Primary stages (3 & 6) drive pooled effects; grade 9 estimate insignificant.

---

Identification Strategy

| Element | Detail | | Design | Staggered rollout quasi-experiment; not randomized assignment to Boost. | | Estimation | Dynamic event study; stacked cohorts; never-treated schools as controls only (Sun & Abraham 2020; Callaway & Sant’Anna 2020). | | Fixed effects | School×implementation wave; calendar time×voucher/municipal×wave. | | Inference | Cluster-robust SEs at school level. | | Student scores | Reduced-form ITT on expected school treatment. | | Teacher surveys | DiD on teachers at treated schools (text: ATT). |

Claim strength: Authors argue causal effects of program rollout on achievement and teacher reports under parallel trends (pre-treatment coefficients near zero; predicted composition unchanged). Distinct from RCT identification in Loyalka/Kozuka. Do not pool effect sizes with those RCTs or compute a meta-analytic mean in this database.

---

Main Findings

A. Empirical — PD → student mathematics achievement (staggered DiD, reduced-form)
  • Primary pooled post years: +0.0263 SD (SE 0.0085, ***), Table 2 Panel B (all stages).
  • By stage (pooled post): Grade 3 +0.0447 SD*; grade 6 +0.0286 SD; grade 9 +0.0044 SD (n.s.).
  • Post windows (all stages): 0–1 years +0.0184; 2–3 years +0.0347; 4–5 years +0.0264 (Panel A).
  • Implementation year (τ = 0): ~+0.012 SD, p = 0.105 (not significant)—separate from pooled post estimate.
B. Empirical — PD → teaching practice (self-report, staggered DiD)
  • Classroom time (Table 7, τ = 0): More time on problem-solving discussion (+1.91 pp, ); less on routine problem work (−3.32 pp, *); more other activities (+1.49 pp, ). Authors argue some shifts persist** in τ = 1–2.
  • Peer collaboration (Table 6): Higher collegial planning and didactic discussion during implementation year; most collaboration measures fade after implementation (unlike persistent time-use shifts).
C. Training hours (not mapped to teacher knowledge)
  • Table 5: Large increases in reported in-service hours (mathematics content, didactics, coaching, collaboration) during implementation year; largely not maintained in later years.
D. Authors’ mechanism narrative (not causal pathway estimates)

Persistent self-reported instructional shifts are discussed as explaining longer-run test score gains; no formal link from practice measures to student scores.

---

What the Paper Establishes

Folder 07 — quasi-experimental PD → achievement and PD → self-reported practice evidence at national scale, explicitly not an RCT and not pooled with Kozuka/Loyalka effect sizes.

Main Findings (summary)

  • Large-scale staggered DiD evaluation of Sweden’s Boost for Mathematics (Lesson study–style PD) finds moderate positive reduced-form effects on national mathematics exam scores (primary +0.0263 SD, SE 0.0085) and changes in self-reported classroom time use and collaboration during implementation—without teacher knowledge tests or estimated practice→achievement pathways.

Workbook + supplemental instructional time confounds pure PD interpretation

Publication

JICA Ogata Research Institute Discussion Paper, No. 43 (October 2025, PDF pp. 1–2).

Discussion paper (not peer-reviewed; assessed by the JICA Ogata Research Institute review committee, PDF p. 2).

Research Question

Does a government-implemented teacher professional development (PD) program (with optional student workbooks and supplemental classes) improve teaching practice, teacher content knowledge, school-based continuous PD activity, and student test scores in mathematics and physics?

Sample / Evidence Base

  • Country: Ethiopia (Oromia Region).
  • Education level: Primary grades 7 and 8.
  • Subject: Mathematics and physics (analyzed separately).
  • Setting: 335 schools planned; school-level randomization into control, Regular PD, or PD-plus-Workbook within 10 strata (zone × one-teacher school); see PDF pp. 8–9 for school counts per arm.
  • Sample (analysis): Teacher outcomes ~141–172 teachers per column (Table 2); student test score regressions 1,356–1,687 student observations depending on subject/wave (Table 5).
  • Timeline: Baseline Nov 2014–Jan 2015; in-service training Feb/Mar/June 2015; workbooks Sep 2015 (workbook arm); midline Nov 2015–Feb 2016; endline late Mar–Apr 2016 (Figure 1, PDF p. 10).

---

Student Achievement Measure

Subject: Mathematics and physics (grade 7–8 primary).

Assessment: Student tests “designed to align with students’ grade levels according to the national curricula and textbooks” (PDF p. 9). Not clearly reported in the paper whether tests were externally developed or identical to a national exam; they are study-administered, curriculum-aligned tests.

Standardization: Test scores normalized so control-group mean = 0 and SD = 1 (Table 5 notes, PDF p. 23).

Modeling: ITT regressions with strata FE, controls, baseline test scores when available; SEs clustered at school level for student scores (PDF pp. 13, 23).

Other student outcomes: Motivation, confidence, engagement—not examined in the reported results.

Workbook arm (achievement interpretation): PD-plus-Workbook includes supplemental classes and teacher monitoring/explanation of exercises (PDF pp. 7–8)—student score gains in that arm cannot be attributed to PD alone or to practice changes alone.

---

Identification Strategy

| Element | Detail | | Design | Randomized controlled trial (school-level assignment). | | Unit of analysis | Teachers (practice, knowledge, school-based PD); students (test scores). | | Temporal structure | Panel-style surveys (baseline, midline, endline); treatment effects at midline/endline. | | Estimation | OLS/linear regression of outcome on Regular PD and PD-plus-Workbook dummies; inverse probability weighting (IPW) for teacher attrition (PDF pp. 13–14). | | Controls | Strata dummies, rural/urban, school size, principal experience, teacher demographics/education/major, student demographics, baseline outcome when available, indicator for schools affected by Dec 2015 demonstrations (PDF pp. 13–14). | | Clustering | School-level clustering for student test scores; robust SEs reported for many teacher outcomes (Table 2 notes vs Table 5 notes). |

Claim strength:

  • Plausibly causal for intent-to-treat effects of assignment to Regular PD vs PD-plus-Workbook vs control, under standard RCT assumptions plus stated handling of attrition.
  • Not causal for links among knowledge, practice, and achievement within the study (those are not identified).
  • Authors’ learning-theory discussion (indirect effect via learning opportunities; PDF p. 33) is interpretation, not an estimated mechanism.

---

Main Findings

A. Empirical — PD → teaching practice (causal ITT)

At endline, Regular PD increased the teaching-practice mean index by 0.582 SD (math) and 0.494 SD (physics); PD-plus-Workbook by 0.637 SD (math) and 0.771 SD (physics)—all p < 0.01 in main specification (Table 2, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 17). Midline effects same direction, smaller (text PDF pp. 15–16). PD-plus-Workbook vs Regular PD in physics at endline: difference 0.277 SD (p = 0.009 for β₂−β₁, Table 2, PDF p. 17).

B. Empirical — PD → teacher content knowledge (causal ITT)

At endline, Regular PD: 0.383 SD (math, p < 0.05), 0.381 SD (physics, p < 0.10); PD-plus-Workbook: 0.479 SD (math, p < 0.05), 0.626 SD (physics, p < 0.01) (Table 3, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 19). At midline, significant effect only for PD-plus-Workbook in mathematics (0.544 SD, p < 0.01; Table 3, PDF p. 19).

C. Empirical — PD → student achievement (causal ITT)

Endline (main specification): Regular PD coefficients 0.004 (math) and −0.064 (physics)—not statistically significant (Table 5, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 23). PD-plus-Workbook: 0.143 SD (math, n.s.), 0.250 SD (physics, p < 0.05) (Table 5, PDF p. 23). Text summarizes: Regular PD alone did not improve scores; PD-plus-Workbook yielded significant impact on physics (PDF p. 22).

Midline: Regular PD math −0.112 (main spec, n.s.); IPW spec −0.146\* (Table 5, PDF p. 23)—authors note this exception (PDF p. 22).

D. Empirical — school-based continuous PD

Both treatment arms increased frequency of collaborative learning / peer activities at midline (large effects vs control means) and smaller but positive endline effects for mathematics (Table 4, PDF pp. 20–21).

E. Heterogeneity (empirical)
  • Less experienced teachers (≤5 years): larger effects on practice and knowledge; workbook arm also shows positive student score effects for less experienced teachers in some columns (Table 6, PDF pp. 25–26).
  • Student baseline quintiles: Regular PD null across quintiles; PD-plus-Workbook significant for higher quintiles in some cells (e.g., math Q4 0.452 SD\\, physics Q4 0.382 SD\, Q5 0.801 SD\, Table 7, PDF p. 27).
F. Authors’ interpretation (not separately identified)

Improved teaching quality may affect achievement indirectly when students have learning opportunities; workbooks may extend time on task and retention (PDF p. 33). Not tested as mediation.

---

What the Paper Establishes

Cite under teacher PD intervention evidence (folder 07): LMIC RCT of government SMASE-style PD with ITT effects on content knowledge, observed practice, and student tests, including null short-run achievement effects for Regular PD and gains in the PD-plus-Workbook arm (with supplemental instruction—not a clean PD-only achievement result).

What the Paper Does Not Establish

That teacher content knowledge or observed teaching practice causes higher student achievement in this setting; that a Knowledge → Practice → Achievement chain was estimated; or that mathematics scores significantly rose from PD alone or from workbooks (main-spec math endline 0.143 SD, n.s., Table 5).

Secondary Relevance

  • Comparison in discussion to null China national PD (Loyalka et al.) vs practice-oriented Ethiopia delivery (PDF pp. 33–34)—authors’ program-design argument, not new data.
  • Heterogeneity: low-performing students gained less from workbooks (Table 7; discussion PDF p. 33).
  • Attrition analysis and bounds (Tables 8–9): teacher practice effects more robust than student score effects under attrition scenarios.

Main Findings (summary)

  • Randomized evaluation in Ethiopia shows government SMASE-style PD substantially improved observed teaching practice and teacher content knowledge, but Regular PD alone did not significantly improve student test scores; PD plus curriculum-aligned workbooks used in supplemental classes produced significant endline gains in physics (and non-significant positive math estimates).

Evidence Mapping

  • PD → Teacher Knowledge: RCT
  • PD → Teaching Practice: RCT
  • PD → Student Achievement: RCT
  • Teacher Knowledge → Teaching Practice: No direct evidence
  • Teaching Practice → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Teacher Knowledge → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Knowledge → Practice → Achievement: No direct evidence

Relevance to My Study

  • LMIC government-system PD (mathematics): Large RCT showing in-service SMASE-style PD can shift observed practice and content knowledge without financial incentives (Tables 2–3).

  • PD intervention, multiple outcomes: Under Regular PD, ITT estimates show teacher-side gains (Tables 2–3) but no significant endline student test gains (Table 5)—useful when discussing PD → achievement in LMIC CPD, not as direct evidence on Practice → Achievement or Knowledge → Achievement.

  • School-based continuation: Evidence that treated teachers increased peer learning/lesson study–type activity after training (Table 4)—relevant to sustained CPD after central training.

Publication

American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, vol. 11, no. 3 (2019).

Large-scale cluster randomized evaluation

Research Question

What are the impacts of a typical large-scale national teacher PD program (and post-training follow-up and content evaluation interventions) on student mathematics achievement, teacher knowledge, teaching-related behaviors, and related outcomes—and why might effects be absent?

Sample / Evidence Base

  • Country: China (rural junior high schools, one large province, 94 counties).
  • Education level: Junior high (grades 7–9).
  • Subject: Mathematics teachers and students.
  • Sample: 300 schools; 600 math teachers (primary + spillover design); ~33,500 students (PDF pp. 3, 8).
  • Intervention: 15-day in-person NTTP (Nov 2015) + online PD; optional SMS/phone follow-up; optional post-training lesson evaluation (PDF pp. 6–7, 13).
  • Randomization: Two-stage cluster RCT (Figure 1, PDF p. 9); 100 schools per arm at first stage; block FE by grade/provider (PDF pp. 8–9).

---

Student Achievement Measure

Subject: Mathematics (junior high, grades 7–9).

Assessment: 35-minute grade-appropriate tests aligned with national and provincial curricula; items from standardized curricula; expert content validity; pilot validation (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.8 for student tests, PDF p. 11). Vertically scaled; same form baseline/midline, different form endline (PDF p. 11).

Scoring: Each wave normalized to control-group mean and SD; effects in SD units (PDF p. 11).

Sample: Primary sample ~16,661 students (+ spillover sample → ~33,492–33,580 total students cited, PDF pp. 3, 8).

Other student outcomes: Dropout, math self-concept, anxiety, motivation, time on math (Table 3)—generally null.

---

Identification Strategy

| Element | Detail | | Design | Cluster randomized trial (school-level assignment). | | Estimation | OLS: \(Y_{ij} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 D_j + X_{ij}\alpha + \tau_k + \varepsilon_{ij}\) (PDF p. 12). | | Outcomes | Student achievement, student reports, teacher knowledge/beliefs, spillovers. | | Controls | Baseline outcome when available; expanded covariate-adjusted specs (PDF p. 13). | | Inference | School clustering (student outcomes); heteroskedastic-robust SEs (teacher outcomes). | | Power | MDES ~0.13 SD (5% level); abstract cites ability to detect ≥0.11 SD (PDF pp. 3, 9). |

Claim strength: Causal ITT for PD → each outcome under RCT assumptions + low attrition (~8% students endline, PDF p. 12). Not causal for relationships among knowledge, practice, and achievement.

---

Main Findings

A. Empirical — PD → student mathematics achievement (ITT)

Endline, covariate-adjusted (Table 2, PDF pp. 14–15):

  • PD vs control: −0.006 SD (SE 0.034), not significant.
  • PD + follow-up vs control: 0.005 SD (SE 0.035), not significant.
  • PD + evaluation vs control: 0.011 SD (SE 0.032), not significant.
  • Upper bound of 95% CI for PD vs control ~0.061 SD—authors rule out large positive effects (PDF p. 15).
B. Empirical — PD → teacher math knowledge (ITT)

Table 5 (PDF p. 18): PD vs control coefficient 0.153 SD (SE 0.138); PD + follow-up 0.222 SD (SE 0.145)—not significant at 10% after multiple-testing adjustment (table note).

C. Empirical — PD → student-reported teaching practice (ITT)

Table 4 (PDF p. 17): PD vs control on teacher practice index 0.043 SD (SE 0.045)—not significant (adjusted for multiple testing per note).

D. Empirical — secondary student outcomes

Table 3: No significant impacts on dropout, math anxiety, motivation, time on math (PDF pp. 16–17).

E. Heterogeneity — PD → achievement (exploratory)

Table 7 (PDF pp. 19–20): Significant interactions by teacher college degree and math major—e.g., PD × college degree −0.203 SD on achievement; some positive effects for students of less qualified teachers (e.g., PD + follow-up +0.097 SD for teachers without four-year college, table note). Authors interpret as substitution away from classroom during training (PDF pp. 19–21).

F. Mechanisms (qualitative + descriptive, not causal mediation)

High participation/attendance but content ~52% theoretical; delivery passive/lecture-heavy; teachers report difficulty applying content (PDF pp. 21–22). Not formal mediation estimates.

G. Authors’ interpretation

Large-scale government PD with typical features (lectures, limited follow-up) failed to improve knowledge, practice, or achievement on average; cautionary for billions spent on similar programs (PDF pp. 3–4, 23).

---

What the Paper Establishes

Folder 07—counterpoint government at-scale PD that does not move knowledge, practice, or achievement jointly; pair with Popova (design gaps) and Kozuka (positive teacher-side ITTs under different PD model).

What the Paper Does Not Establish

That teacher knowledge causes practice or achievement; that pathway mediation was tested; or that PD always fails (heterogeneity for less qualified teachers; different contexts).

Main Findings (summary)

  • Cluster RCT of China’s National Teacher Training Program shows precise null ITTs on student math achievement, teacher math knowledge for teaching, and student-reported teaching behaviors, with qualitative evidence that PD was too theoretical and passively delivered.

  • PD → Student Achievement: RCT
  • Teacher Knowledge → Teaching Practice: No direct evidence
  • Teaching Practice → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Teacher Knowledge → Student Achievement: No direct evidence

Publication

The World Bank Research Observer, vol. 37, no. 1 (2022).

Cross-study synthesis + descriptive survey + exploratory correlational analysis.

Research Question

How do design and implementation characteristics of in-service teacher PD relate to student learning gains in rigorously evaluated LMIC programs, and how do government-funded, at-scale PD programs compare to high-impact evaluated programs on those characteristics?

Sample / Evidence Base

Evaluated programs (N=33): From structured literature search (2016, updated 2018): impact evaluations in LMIC, primary/secondary, in-service PD (major component), student test scores in math/language/science (PDF pp. 8–9, Figure 2). Coded from papers + implementer interviews (18/33; PDF pp. 9–11).

At-scale programs: 14 countries; rosters of government-funded PD 2012–2016; sample of 10 largest programs per country/state by teacher coverage; ITTSI on 2 largest primary + 1 secondary (+ BITTSI on 7 others) (PDF pp. 15–16).

---

Main Findings

A. Produced by this paper’s analysis (associational, evaluated sample)

Effectiveness defined: standardized gains in student test scores from underlying impact evaluations.

Organization (robust associations, Table 2, PDF pp. 12–13, 17–18):

  • No career implications for participation → −0.12 SD student learning vs programs with incentives (p<0.05); inverse: linking PD to promotion/salary/status associated with higher impacts.
  • Targeting by years of experience → +0.10 SD (p<0.10); driven by two programs only (PDF p. 17).

Content (Table 3, PDF pp. 13, 18):

  • No subject focus → −0.24 SD (p<0.01).
  • Counseling focus → −0.20 SD (p<0.01); specific tool focus negative (robust in table).
  • Lesson enactment in training → +0.10 SD (p<0.10, robust).

Delivery (Table 4, PDF pp. 14, 18–19):

  • Initial consecutive days of face-to-face training → +0.14 SD (p<0.01, robust).
  • Training at central location (hotel/conference) vs omitted categories → −0.13 SD (p<0.10, robust).
  • Distance learning → −0.10 SD (p<0.10, robust).
  • Follow-up visits to review material +0.14 SD (large coefficient, not significant); practice with other teachers correlated with impacts (text, not always significant).

Qualitative (18 implementers, PDF pp. 19–20): Mentoring follow-up visits and complementary materials (e.g., structured lessons) most often cited as crucial; teachers liked interactive/fun sessions and materials; disliked time burden.

B. Synthesized descriptive gap (at-scale vs top performers, Table 5, PDF pp. 21–22)
  • Incentives: 88% top performers vs 58% at-scale link PD to status/salary/promotion.
  • Materials: storybooks/reading materials less common at-scale (e.g., storybooks 12.5% vs 36% top performers).
  • Follow-up: median 3.5 vs 0 visits (among programs with visits); ~85% vs ~50% include follow-up visits.
  • Practice with other teachers: ~40% vs ~16% of training time (top vs at-scale).
  • Many at-scale programs still have subject focus, face-to-face blocks, lesson enactment; gaps are sharpest on incentives, follow-up, practice time, reading materials (PDF pp. 19–22).
C. Authors’ interpretation / recommendations (not new causal evidence)
  • Standardize reporting via ITTSI; scale PD should adopt features of successful evaluated programs (incentives, practice, follow-up) (PDF pp. 22–23, 25).
  • Scale mismatch: evaluated programs smaller (~96 schools vs >6,000 at-scale), often researcher-designed; scaling may require cutting costly elements (PDF pp. 24–25).
  • Limitations: small evaluated N, bivariate only, selection of which programs were evaluated, possible Hawthorne/observer effects (PDF pp. 23–24).

---

What the Paper Establishes

Foundation / framing for LMIC teacher PD and CPD design, implementation at scale, and why PD effects on student achievement vary—alongside single-program RCTs (folder 07), not as direct evidence on knowledge → practice → achievement.

What the Paper Does Not Establish

Causal effects of PD features; that PD improves teacher knowledge or teaching practice in their data; that practice → achievement is estimated; or that your school-based CPD will raise achievement because it includes a listed feature.

Secondary Relevance

  • Conceptual links among PD content, teacher skills, and student learning in background sections.
  • Bold et al. (2017) teacher knowledge gaps in Africa cited in introduction (PDF p. 2)—background only.

Main Findings (summary)

  • ITTSI + cross-study associational analysis (33 LMIC PD evaluations) and descriptive comparison to 139 at-scale government PD programs, documenting a gap between design features associated with student learning gains and typical at-scale practice.

Evidence Mapping

  • PD → Teacher Knowledge: No direct evidence
  • PD → Teaching Practice: No direct evidence
  • PD → Student Achievement: Cross-study synthesis
  • Teacher Knowledge → Teaching Practice: No direct evidence
  • Teaching Practice → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Teacher Knowledge → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Knowledge → Practice → Achievement: No direct evidence

Relevance to My Study

  • Defining and comparing PD/CPD in LMIC government systems vs research-supported design features (incentives, subject specificity, enactment, face-to-face intensity, follow-up, peer practice, materials)—for Theory of Change and implementation discussion.

  • Explaining heterogeneous PD effects on student achievement in the literature: this paper argues much variation is linked to measurable program design, not only “PD yes/no” (PDF pp. 2–3).

  • School-based / embedded PD: authors theorize school-based delivery as effective (PDF p. 4); ITTSI includes location and follow-up indicators; at-scale programs often use central venues (PDF p. 22)—relevant to school-based CPD design choices.

  • Pairing with Kozuka (2025): Popova cites Loyalka et al. (2019) null China PD and discusses lesson enactment, face-to-face, follow-up—helps interpret why government at-scale PD may improve some inputs yet show weak achievement effects in single-program RCTs.
