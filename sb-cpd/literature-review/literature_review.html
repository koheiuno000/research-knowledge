Research Question

Does a government-implemented teacher professional development (PD) program (with optional student workbooks and supplemental classes) improve **teaching practice**, **teacher content knowledge**, **school-based continuous PD activity**, and **student test scores** in mathematics and physics?

Sample / Evidence Base

- **Country:** Ethiopia (Oromia Region). - **Education level:** Primary **grades 7 and 8**. - **Subject:** Mathematics and physics (analyzed separately). - **Setting:** 335 schools planned; school-level randomization into control, Regular PD, or PD-plus-Workbook within 10 strata (zone × one-teacher school); see PDF pp. 8–9 for school counts per arm. - **Sample (analysis):** Teacher outcomes ~141–172 teachers per column (Table 2); student test score regressions **1,356–1,687** student observations depending on subject/wave (Table 5). - **Timeline:** Baseline Nov 2014–Jan 2015; in-service training Feb/Mar/June 2015; workbooks Sep 2015 (workbook arm); midline Nov 2015–Feb 2016; endline late Mar–Apr 2016 (Figure 1, PDF p. 10). ---

Student Achievement Measure

**Subject:** Mathematics and physics (grade 7–8 primary). **Assessment:** Student tests **“designed to align with students’ grade levels according to the national curricula and textbooks”** (PDF p. 9). **Not clearly reported in the paper** whether tests were externally developed or identical to a national exam; they are study-administered, curriculum-aligned tests. **Standardization:** Test scores **normalized** so control-group mean = 0 and SD = 1 (Table 5 notes, PDF p. 23). **Modeling:** ITT regressions with strata FE, controls, baseline test scores when available; **SEs clustered at school level** for student scores (PDF pp. 13, 23). **Other student outcomes:** Motivation, confidence, engagement—**not examined** in the reported results. **Workbook arm (achievement interpretation):** PD-plus-Workbook includes **supplemental classes** and teacher monitoring/explanation of exercises (PDF pp. 7–8)—student score gains in that arm **cannot be attributed to PD alone** or to practice changes alone. ---

Identification Strategy

| Element | Detail | | Design | **Randomized controlled trial** (school-level assignment). | | Unit of analysis | Teachers (practice, knowledge, school-based PD); students (test scores). | | Temporal structure | **Panel-style surveys** (baseline, midline, endline); treatment effects at midline/endline. | | Estimation | OLS/linear regression of outcome on Regular PD and PD-plus-Workbook dummies; **inverse probability weighting (IPW)** for teacher attrition (PDF pp. 13–14). | | Controls | Strata dummies, rural/urban, school size, principal experience, teacher demographics/education/major, student demographics, baseline outcome when available, indicator for schools affected by Dec 2015 demonstrations (PDF pp. 13–14). | | Clustering | School-level clustering for **student** test scores; robust SEs reported for many teacher outcomes (Table 2 notes vs Table 5 notes). | **Claim strength:** - **Plausibly causal** for **intent-to-treat effects of assignment** to Regular PD vs PD-plus-Workbook vs control, under standard RCT assumptions plus stated handling of attrition. - **Not causal** for links among knowledge, practice, and achievement **within** the study (those are not identified). - Authors’ **learning-theory discussion** (indirect effect via learning opportunities; PDF p. 33) is **interpretation**, not an estimated mechanism. ---

Main Findings

### A. Empirical — PD → teaching practice (causal ITT) At **endline**, Regular PD increased the teaching-practice mean index by **0.582 SD (math)** and **0.494 SD (physics)**; PD-plus-Workbook by **0.637 SD (math)** and **0.771 SD (physics)**—all **p < 0.01** in main specification (Table 2, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 17). Midline effects same direction, smaller (text PDF pp. 15–16). PD-plus-Workbook **vs** Regular PD in physics at endline: difference **0.277 SD** (p = 0.009 for β₂−β₁, Table 2, PDF p. 17). ### B. Empirical — PD → teacher content knowledge (causal ITT) At **endline**, Regular PD: **0.383 SD (math, p < 0.05)**, **0.381 SD (physics, p < 0.10)**; PD-plus-Workbook: **0.479 SD (math, p < 0.05)**, **0.626 SD (physics, p < 0.01)** (Table 3, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 19). At midline, significant effect **only** for PD-plus-Workbook in **mathematics** (0.544 SD, p < 0.01; Table 3, PDF p. 19). ### C. Empirical — PD → student achievement (causal ITT) **Endline (main specification):** Regular PD coefficients **0.004 (math)** and **−0.064 (physics)**—not statistically significant (Table 5, cols. 3–4, PDF p. 23). PD-plus-Workbook: **0.143 SD (math, n.s.)**, **0.250 SD (physics, p < 0.05)** (Table 5, PDF p. 23). Text summarizes: Regular PD alone did not improve scores; PD-plus-Workbook yielded significant impact on **physics** (PDF p. 22). **Midline:** Regular PD math **−0.112** (main spec, n.s.); IPW spec **−0.146\*** (Table 5, PDF p. 23)—authors note this exception (PDF p. 22). ### D. Empirical — school-based continuous PD Both treatment arms increased frequency of **collaborative learning / peer activities** at midline (large effects vs control means) and smaller but positive endline effects for mathematics (Table 4, PDF pp. 20–21). ### E. Heterogeneity (empirical) - **Less experienced teachers (≤5 years):** larger effects on practice and knowledge; workbook arm also shows positive student score effects for less experienced teachers in some columns (Table 6, PDF pp. 25–26). - **Student baseline quintiles:** Regular PD null across quintiles; PD-plus-Workbook **significant for higher quintiles** in some cells (e.g., math Q4 **0.452 SD\*\***, physics Q4 **0.382 SD\***, Q5 **0.801 SD\***, Table 7, PDF p. 27). ### F. Authors’ interpretation (not separately identified) Improved teaching quality may affect achievement **indirectly** when students have **learning opportunities**; workbooks may extend time on task and retention (PDF p. 33). **Not tested** as mediation. ---

What the Paper Establishes

Cite under **teacher PD intervention evidence** (folder 07): **LMIC RCT** of government **SMASE-style PD** with ITT effects on **content knowledge**, **observed practice**, and **student tests**, including **null short-run achievement effects for Regular PD** and gains in the **PD-plus-Workbook** arm (with supplemental instruction—not a clean PD-only achievement result).

What the Paper Does Not Establish

That **teacher content knowledge** or **observed teaching practice** **causes** higher student achievement in this setting; that a **Knowledge → Practice → Achievement** chain was **estimated**; or that **mathematics** scores significantly rose from PD alone or from workbooks (main-spec math endline **0.143 SD**, n.s., Table 5).

Secondary Relevance

- Comparison in discussion to **null China national PD** (Loyalka et al.) vs **practice-oriented Ethiopia delivery** (PDF pp. 33–34)—authors’ program-design argument, not new data. - Heterogeneity: **low-performing students** gained less from workbooks (Table 7; discussion PDF p. 33). - Attrition analysis and bounds (Tables 8–9): teacher practice effects more robust than student score effects under attrition scenarios.

Main Findings (summary)

  • Randomized evaluation in Ethiopia shows government **SMASE-style PD** substantially improved **observed teaching practice** and **teacher content knowledge**, but **Regular PD alone did not significantly improve student test scores**; **PD plus curriculum-aligned workbooks used in supplemental classes** produced **significant endline gains in physics** (and non-significant positive math estimates).

Evidence Mapping

  • PD → Teaching Practice: RCT
  • PD → Student Achievement: RCT
  • Teacher Knowledge → Teaching Practice: No direct evidence
  • Teaching Practice → Student Achievement: No direct evidence
  • Teacher Knowledge → Student Achievement: No direct evidence

Relevance to My Study

  • **LMIC government-system PD (mathematics):** Large RCT showing **in-service SMASE-style PD can shift observed practice and content knowledge** without financial incentives (Tables 2–3).
  • **PD intervention, multiple outcomes:** Under **Regular PD**, ITT estimates show teacher-side gains (Tables 2–3) but **no significant endline student test gains** (Table 5)—useful when discussing **PD → achievement** in LMIC CPD, not as direct evidence on Practice → Achievement or Knowledge → Achievement.
  • **School-based continuation:** Evidence that treated teachers increased **peer learning/lesson study–type activity** after training (Table 4)—relevant to **sustained CPD** after central training.

Research Question

What are the **impacts** of a typical large-scale **national teacher PD program** (and post-training **follow-up** and **content evaluation** interventions) on **student mathematics achievement**, **teacher knowledge**, **teaching-related behaviors**, and related outcomes—and **why** might effects be absent?

Sample / Evidence Base

- **Country:** China (rural junior high schools, one large province, 94 counties). - **Education level:** Junior high (**grades 7–9**). - **Subject:** **Mathematics** teachers and students. - **Sample:** **300 schools**; **600 math teachers** (primary + spillover design); **~33,500 students** (PDF pp. 3, 8). - **Intervention:** 15-day **in-person** NTTP (Nov 2015) + **online** PD; optional **SMS/phone follow-up**; optional **post-training lesson evaluation** (PDF pp. 6–7, 13). - **Randomization:** Two-stage cluster RCT (Figure 1, PDF p. 9); **100 schools per arm** at first stage; block FE by grade/provider (PDF pp. 8–9). ---

Student Achievement Measure

**Subject:** **Mathematics** (junior high, grades 7–9). **Assessment:** **35-minute** grade-appropriate tests aligned with **national and provincial curricula**; items from standardized curricula; expert content validity; pilot validation (Cronbach’s α ≈ 0.8 for student tests, PDF p. 11). **Vertically scaled**; same form baseline/midline, **different form endline** (PDF p. 11). **Scoring:** Each wave **normalized** to control-group mean and SD; effects in **SD units** (PDF p. 11). **Sample:** Primary sample **~16,661 students** (+ spillover sample → **~33,492–33,580** total students cited, PDF pp. 3, 8). **Other student outcomes:** Dropout, math self-concept, anxiety, motivation, time on math (Table 3)—generally **null**. ---

Identification Strategy

| Element | Detail | | Design | **Cluster randomized trial** (school-level assignment). | | Estimation | OLS: \(Y_{ij} = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 D_j + X_{ij}\alpha + \tau_k + \varepsilon_{ij}\) (PDF p. 12). | | Outcomes | Student achievement, student reports, teacher knowledge/beliefs, spillovers. | | Controls | Baseline outcome when available; expanded covariate-adjusted specs (PDF p. 13). | | Inference | School clustering (student outcomes); heteroskedastic-robust SEs (teacher outcomes). | | Power | MDES **~0.13 SD** (5% level); abstract cites ability to detect **≥0.11 SD** (PDF pp. 3, 9). | **Claim strength:** **Causal ITT** for **PD → each outcome** under RCT assumptions + low attrition (~8% students endline, PDF p. 12). **Not causal** for relationships among knowledge, practice, and achievement. ---

Main Findings

### A. Empirical — PD → student mathematics achievement (ITT) **Endline, covariate-adjusted (Table 2, PDF pp. 14–15):** - **PD vs control:** **−0.006 SD** (SE 0.034), not significant. - **PD + follow-up vs control:** **0.005 SD** (SE 0.035), not significant. - **PD + evaluation vs control:** **0.011 SD** (SE 0.032), not significant. - Upper bound of 95% CI for PD vs control **~0.061 SD**—authors rule out large positive effects (PDF p. 15). ### B. Empirical — PD → teacher math knowledge (ITT) **Table 5 (PDF p. 18):** PD vs control coefficient **0.153 SD** (SE 0.138); PD + follow-up **0.222 SD** (SE 0.145)—**not significant** at 10% after multiple-testing adjustment (table note). ### C. Empirical — PD → student-reported teaching practice (ITT) **Table 4 (PDF p. 17):** PD vs control on **teacher practice** index **0.043 SD** (SE 0.045)—**not significant** (adjusted for multiple testing per note). ### D. Empirical — secondary student outcomes **Table 3:** No significant impacts on dropout, math anxiety, motivation, time on math (PDF pp. 16–17). ### E. Heterogeneity — PD → achievement (exploratory) **Table 7 (PDF pp. 19–20):** Significant interactions by **teacher college degree** and **math major**—e.g., PD × college degree **−0.203 SD** on achievement; some **positive** effects for students of **less qualified** teachers (e.g., PD + follow-up **+0.097 SD** for teachers without four-year college, table note). Authors interpret as **substitution away from classroom** during training (PDF pp. 19–21). ### F. Mechanisms (qualitative + descriptive, not causal mediation) High **participation/attendance** but content **~52% theoretical**; delivery **passive/lecture-heavy**; teachers report difficulty applying content (PDF pp. 21–22). **Not** formal mediation estimates. ### G. Authors’ interpretation Large-scale **government PD** with typical features (lectures, limited follow-up) **failed to improve** knowledge, practice, or achievement on average; cautionary for billions spent on similar programs (PDF pp. 3–4, 23). ---

What the Paper Establishes

**Folder 07**—counterpoint **government at-scale PD** that **does not** move knowledge, practice, or achievement jointly; pair with **Popova** (design gaps) and **Kozuka** (positive teacher-side ITTs under different PD model).

What the Paper Does Not Establish

That teacher knowledge **causes** practice or achievement; that **pathway mediation** was tested; or that PD **always** fails (heterogeneity for less qualified teachers; different contexts).

Main Findings (summary)

  • **Cluster RCT** of China’s **National Teacher Training Program** shows **precise null ITTs** on **student math achievement**, **teacher math knowledge for teaching**, and **student-reported teaching behaviors**, with qualitative evidence that PD was **too theoretical** and **passively delivered**.

Research Question

How do **design and implementation characteristics** of in-service teacher PD relate to **student learning gains** in rigorously evaluated LMIC programs, and how do **government-funded, at-scale** PD programs compare to **high-impact evaluated** programs on those characteristics?

Sample / Evidence Base

**Evaluated programs (N=33):** From structured literature search (2016, updated 2018): impact evaluations in LMIC, primary/secondary, in-service PD (major component), **student test scores** in math/language/science (PDF pp. 8–9, Figure 2). Coded from papers + implementer interviews (18/33; PDF pp. 9–11). **At-scale programs:** 14 countries; rosters of government-funded PD 2012–2016; sample of **10 largest** programs per country/state by teacher coverage; ITTSI on 2 largest primary + 1 secondary (+ BITTSI on 7 others) (PDF pp. 15–16). ---

Main Findings

### A. Produced by this paper’s analysis (associational, evaluated sample) **Effectiveness defined:** standardized **gains in student test scores** from underlying impact evaluations. **Organization (robust associations, Table 2, PDF pp. 12–13, 17–18):** - **No** career implications for participation → **−0.12 SD** student learning vs programs with incentives (p<0.05); inverse: linking PD to promotion/salary/status associated with higher impacts. - **Targeting by years of experience** → **+0.10 SD** (p<0.10); driven by **two programs** only (PDF p. 17). **Content (Table 3, PDF pp. 13, 18):** - **No subject focus** → **−0.24 SD** (p<0.01). - **Counseling focus** → **−0.20 SD** (p<0.01); **specific tool focus** negative (robust in table). - **Lesson enactment** in training → **+0.10 SD** (p<0.10, robust). **Delivery (Table 4, PDF pp. 14, 18–19):** - **Initial consecutive days of face-to-face training** → **+0.14 SD** (p<0.01, robust). - Training at **central location** (hotel/conference) vs omitted categories → **−0.13 SD** (p<0.10, robust). - **Distance learning** → **−0.10 SD** (p<0.10, robust). - **Follow-up visits to review material** +0.14 SD (large coefficient, **not** significant); **practice with other teachers** correlated with impacts (text, not always significant). **Qualitative (18 implementers, PDF pp. 19–20):** **Mentoring follow-up visits** and **complementary materials** (e.g., structured lessons) most often cited as crucial; teachers liked interactive/fun sessions and materials; disliked **time burden**. ### B. Synthesized descriptive gap (at-scale vs top performers, Table 5, PDF pp. 21–22) - **Incentives:** 88% top performers vs **58%** at-scale link PD to status/salary/promotion. - **Materials:** storybooks/reading materials less common at-scale (e.g., storybooks **12.5%** vs **36%** top performers). - **Follow-up:** median **3.5** vs **0** visits (among programs with visits); **~85%** vs **~50%** include follow-up visits. - **Practice with other teachers:** **~40%** vs **~16%** of training time (top vs at-scale). - Many at-scale programs still have subject focus, face-to-face blocks, lesson enactment; gaps are sharpest on **incentives**, **follow-up**, **practice time**, **reading materials** (PDF pp. 19–22). ### C. Authors’ interpretation / recommendations (not new causal evidence) - Standardize reporting via **ITTSI**; scale PD should adopt features of successful evaluated programs (incentives, practice, follow-up) (PDF pp. 22–23, 25). - **Scale mismatch:** evaluated programs smaller (~96 schools vs >6,000 at-scale), often researcher-designed; scaling may require cutting costly elements (PDF pp. 24–25). - Limitations: small evaluated **N**, bivariate only, selection of which programs were evaluated, possible **Hawthorne/observer effects** (PDF pp. 23–24). ---

What the Paper Establishes

**Foundation / framing** for **LMIC teacher PD and CPD design**, **implementation at scale**, and **why PD effects on student achievement vary**—alongside **single-program RCTs** (folder 07), **not** as direct evidence on knowledge → practice → achievement.

What the Paper Does Not Establish

Causal effects of PD features; that PD improves **teacher knowledge** or **teaching practice** in their data; that **practice → achievement** is estimated; or that **your** school-based CPD will raise achievement because it includes a listed feature.

Secondary Relevance

- Conceptual links among **PD content**, **teacher skills**, and **student learning** in background sections. - **Bold et al. (2017)** teacher knowledge gaps in Africa cited in introduction (PDF p. 2)—background only.

Main Findings (summary)

  • **ITTSI** + **cross-study associational analysis** (33 LMIC PD evaluations) and **descriptive comparison** to **139 at-scale government PD programs**, documenting a **gap** between design features associated with **student learning gains** and typical **at-scale** practice.

Relevance to My Study

  • **Defining and comparing PD/CPD** in LMIC **government systems** vs research-supported design features (incentives, subject specificity, enactment, face-to-face intensity, follow-up, peer practice, materials)—for **Theory of Change** and **implementation** discussion.
  • **Explaining heterogeneous PD effects** on **student achievement** in the literature: this paper argues much variation is linked to **measurable program design**, not only “PD yes/no” (PDF pp. 2–3).
  • **School-based / embedded PD:** authors theorize school-based delivery as effective (PDF p. 4); ITTSI includes location and follow-up indicators; at-scale programs often use **central** venues (PDF p. 22)—relevant to **school-based CPD** design choices.
  • **Pairing with Kozuka (2025):** Popova cites **Loyalka et al. (2019)** null China PD and discusses **lesson enactment**, **face-to-face**, **follow-up**—helps interpret why **government at-scale** PD may improve some inputs yet show **weak achievement effects** in single-program RCTs.
